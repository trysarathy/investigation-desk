WORKING PAPER v0.3 · 29 OCTOBER 2026 · AASHMIIN KAUR

Clearline

A stablecoin transfer is not a payment. Clearline closes the gap between "money moved" and "invoice closed".

The pain

Businesses now get paid in USDC and USDT straight into their own wallets. The chain records that tokens moved. It does not say which invoice they pay, whether the token is real, or whether the amount is right.

Mistakes are expensive. In December 2025 one sender lost 49,999,950 USDT to an address that matched the real one in its first three and last four characters.{{V}}[[poison]] Fake tokens named "USDT" are cheap to make; one we found spells it with look-alike Cyrillic and accented letters: "ÚЅDТ".{{V:demo/REPORT.md}}

What Clearline does

Instruction: one payment page per invoice, with the exact amount, token contract, network and address. Verification: checks the transfer is genuine, complete and final. Matching: ties it to one invoice, never on amount alone. Explanation: says what went wrong in plain English and drafts the reply. Accounting: prepares the entries, which post only after a person approves.{{V:src/lib/requests/lifecycle.ts}}

It reads public chain data and never holds funds or wallet keys.{{V:src/lib/chain/}}

Why now

Real-economy stablecoin payments reached an estimated US$350–550 billion in 2025 (BCG), about 1% of gross stablecoin transfers.{{V}}[[reap]] That slice is commerce, and it needs books that are right.

Rules are arriving where our first customers are. Hong Kong licensed its first stablecoin issuers in April 2026,{{V}}[[hklic]] and MAS consulted on stablecoin amendments to Singapore's Payment Services Act in September 2026.{{V}}[[gd]]

What's built today
  • Payment pages for USDC and USDT on Ethereum, with a look-alike address warning
  • Deterministic checks, conservative matching and plain-English explanations
  • Posting plans that post only after approval; a Xero adapter tested against recorded API shapes
  • A demo with seven payment situations; live mode checks a real Ethereum transaction when someone pastes its ID
What we're testing next
  • Xero's Demo Company, using Xero's 2026 granular permissions
  • Payment pages on Tron
  • Watching wallets, so nobody has to paste a transaction ID
  • First paid pilots in Singapore and Hong Kong
What I'm asking for
  • Blunt feedback on the thesis and the wedge
  • Introductions to finance teams that receive USDC or USDT directly
  • Views on Request Finance and HitPay from people who use them

Section 1

1The problem

It is 9:40 on a Tuesday. Mei runs finance at a 30-person agency in Singapore. Her inbox has an email from a client: "We paid yesterday. Why is invoice INV-1042 still open?"

She opens a block explorer and searches the client's name. Nothing, because the chain doesn't know names. She asks for the transaction ID, waits an hour, and finds it: 4,950 USDC, not 5,000. Was it a fee? A partial payment? A mistake? She checks the token contract against Circle's, writes a polite email, marks the invoice by hand and pastes the explorer link into a note for the auditors. Forty minutes, for one payment that was nearly right.

An illustrative scene, not a customer interview. Mei and her agency are fictional.

A blockchain records transfers: token X moved from A to B. A business needs payments: customer C settled invoice I, in full, in money it can use. Five facts separate the two, and the chain holds none of them: which invoice, genuine or not, complete or not, which network, final or not.

49,999,950 USDT

On 19 December 2025 a sender made a small test payment to the right address, then minutes later sent the full amount to a look-alike that shared the first three and last four characters.{{V}}[[poison]] The transaction is public.{{V}}[[poisontx]] The address came from the sender's own wallet history, not from an invoice. A payee-side tool like Clearline would only have helped if the sender had used the payee's payment page. The lesson stands: people check the ends of an address, and attackers know it.

Counterfeits are the quieter trick. Among the real public transactions we collected to test Clearline's checking engine, 13 were tokens posing as USDT.{{V:demo/REPORT.md}} That reflects how we picked cases, not how common fakes are; each looked fine at a glance.

Section 2

2Why now

The money is real, and still small next to the noise.

Total stablecoin market capitalisation was US$308.0 billion on 13 August 2026. USDT holds about 59% of supply and USDC about 23%. By network, Ethereum carries about 49% and Tron about 31%.{{V}}[[reap]]

Estimates of real-economy payments for goods and services in 2025 run from US$350–550 billion (BCG) to about US$390 billion (McKinsey/Artemis). That is about 1% of gross stablecoin transfers, which measure US$28–62 trillion or more depending on the method: about US$28 trillion per the BIS, more than US$62 trillion per BCG with Allium.{{V}}[[reap]] Most transfer volume is not commerce. McKinsey puts the B2B payment base at about US$226 billion a year,{{V}}[[reap]] and Bessemer estimates that 60% of 2025's real-world stablecoin payments were B2B.{{V}}[[bvp]]

Adoption is early. In a mid-2025 EY-Parthenon survey of 350 decision-makers, about 13% of organisations had used stablecoins (8% of corporates). Paying suppliers cross-border (62%) and accepting cross-border payments (53%) led the use cases.{{V}}[[reap]]

Section 4

4The solution

Five steps, from the invoice to the books. Each one answers one of the five missing facts.

  1. Instruction. Each invoice gets a payment page: the exact amount, the token and its issuer's contract address, the network, the business's own receiving address, and a QR code in the EIP-681 format.{{V:src/lib/requests/present.ts}}[[eip681]] The payer can paste the address their wallet is about to use; Clearline warns them if it is a look-alike.
  2. Verification. Clearline looks the transfer up on Ethereum by its transaction ID, read-only, and checks it succeeded, went to the right address on the right network, came from the issuer's own contract and has 12 confirmations.{{V:src/lib/requests/lifecycle.ts}} Today a person gives it the transaction ID, usually from the customer's email; in the demo, transfers arrive on their own.{{V:src/lib/requests/provider.ts}}
  3. Matching. A transfer belongs to a request when exactly one open request uses its receiving address. If several share it, a person decides, helped by an evidence score.{{V:src/lib/requests/lifecycle.ts}}
  4. Explanation. Templates built from the records say what happened, the likely cause (labelled as an inference), the next step, and a reply to the customer that promises nothing it can't keep.{{V:src/lib/requests/present.ts}}
  5. Accounting. Clearline builds a posting plan: the exact lines that would go into the books. A person approves that plan; then it posts. Counterfeit, failed and wrong-network transfers never post.{{V:src/lib/accounting/rules.ts}}

Worked example: INV-1042

The invoice is for 5,000.00 USD. Clearline imports it and makes a payment page asking for 5,000.00 USDC on Ethereum. The customer pays from an exchange, which takes its fee from the amount, so 4,950.00 USDC arrives. Clearline checks the contract (Circle's), the network (Ethereum), the address (this invoice's) and the confirmations (12 of 12). The verdict: short by 50.00, or 1.0%. Because it came from an exchange's withdrawal wallet and the gap is small, the likely cause is an exchange fee, and the page says that is an inference, not a fact. A polite request for the balance is drafted.{{V:src/lib/requests/lifecycle.ts}}

The finance team decides to absorb it and writes why. The posting plan changes: a payment of 4,950.00 and a separate fee write-off of 50.00, so the invoice closes and the books never show more than arrived.{{V:tests/accounting.test.ts}} The team approves that exact plan, and every step lands in the audit log.

Section 5

5How it actually works

Rules decide. People confirm. Nothing is guessed silently.

The excerpts are real code, trimmed for reading ("…" marks lines or long labels left out; nothing is reworded), from pull requests 31 and 36, both not yet merged.

Every rule runs on every transfer, in a fixed order: failed, issuer freeze and wrong network come before token and address problems. If a chain doesn't answer, the check pauses and concludes nothing rather than calling a payment "not found".{{V:src/lib/engine/run.ts}}

The issuer's contract is fixed when the request is made. A token that calls itself USDT from any other contract is counterfeit; one with a different name is the wrong token. Neither ever counts or posts.{{V:src/lib/requests/lifecycle.ts}}

Address poisoning works because people check the ends. If the pasted address shares the first or last four characters with the real one but isn't it, the payer is told to stop. It warns; it can't block a wallet.{{V:src/lib/requests/lifecycle.ts}}

When several open requests share an address, an equal amount is only a hint. For a payment with no usable reference, Clearline scores the candidates: exact amount owed 40, a sender this customer used before 35, no other request for that amount 15, paid after the invoice 10. A person confirms with a written reason.{{V:src/lib/requests/lifecycle.ts}}

Some transfers can never be booked, whatever anyone clicks. And a person approves one exact plan, identified by its key: if a new transfer or decision changes the plan, approval is refused and the new lines are shown.{{V:src/lib/accounting/engine.ts (PR #36)}}

Finality and valuation

Clearline waits for 12 confirmations on Ethereum, a setting fixed in the code;{{V:src/lib/requests/check.ts}} we plan to use Ethereum's finalized tag, about two epochs (12–13 minutes), and handle reorganisations.{{V}}[[finality]] USDC and USDT are booked at par into a bank-type account, each line saying "no price feed connected"; a price more than 0.5% from par stops automatic posting.{{V:src/lib/accounting/rules.ts}} How to classify a stablecoin is still open: the FASB proposed in August 2026 how some can meet the cash-equivalent definition, with comments closing on 19 November.{{V}}[[fasb]] We have found no settled IFRS or SFRS(I) guidance,{{A}} so par is a default the customer's accountant must accept.

Section 6

6Trust and security

Clearline can look and prepare. It cannot move money.

Read-only, no custody

Clearline has no wallet, no private keys and no signing code. The receiving address belongs to the business; Clearline only reads it.{{V:src/lib/chain/}} This is also our regulatory position. If Clearline received, held or forwarded funds, it would become a party to the payment and in Singapore would likely need a licence for digital payment token services.{{A}} HitPay shows where the line sits: its licensed partner handles the stablecoins, and HitPay does not.{{V}}[[hitpay]] We will take legal advice before charging anyone.

Controls that exist today

  • Decisions, activity, check snapshots and the posting audit are only ever appended. Requests and transfer records update as facts arrive, and each transfer keeps its earlier attributions.{{V:src/lib/requests/lifecycle.ts}}
  • One record per chain:hash:logIndex, so a repeated event never becomes a second payment; one idempotency key per posting line, so a retry never posts twice.{{V:tests/accounting.test.ts}}
  • Every decision needs a written reason. Every approval, posting, failure and refusal is logged.{{V:src/lib/accounting/engine.ts}}

Sanctions

Clearline does not screen against OFAC or UN lists, or use Chainalysis or TRM, and claims no compliance screening. A freeze on the sender barely matters for receipts: a frozen address can't send.{{A}} The real risks are the business's own address being frozen, which Clearline checks on lookup, and tainted funds, which it does not detect. Screening stays with the business for now; an analytics integration is planned.

Data and AI

Clearline stores invoice and customer references, amounts, addresses, transfer records and typed user names; no keys, no identity documents. AI explanations are optional and off in the Docker image, but the code turns them on whenever an Anthropic API key is set; the verdict's evidence, with customer IDs removed, is then sent to Anthropic's API.{{V:src/lib/ai/anthropic.ts}} For pilots, AI stays off unless the customer agrees in writing.

Liability

No funds move through Clearline, and a person approves every posting. Our intended terms limit liability to fees paid, and we need professional indemnity insurance before real volume.{{A}}

Section 7

7The open standard

The deeper fix is shared: an instruction the payer's wallet can verify before sending, and a receipt the payee's auditor can verify after. Prior art points the way. EIP-681 encodes a token transfer as a URL, and Clearline's QR codes use it.{{V}}[[eip681]] Solana Pay adds a reference key so a payee can find its payment.{{V}}[[solpay]] Request Network logs a reference on-chain.{{V}}[[rnpn]] ERC-7699 proposes a reference field for ERC-20 transfers.{{V}}[[erc7699]]

The BIP70 lesson. Bitcoin tried signed payment requests tied to the web's certificate system. It "never saw widespread adoption", pulled in outside security dependencies, and was removed from Bitcoin Core in 2019.{{V}}[[bip70]] So we start from what wallets already read (EIP-681), add a signature only where a wallet team agrees to verify it, and call nothing a standard until real customers use it.

Section 9

9Business model and path

Who pays first

Our hypothesis: the founder or finance lead of a small or mid-sized business in Singapore or Hong Kong that invoices in USD and is paid in USDT or USDC straight into its own wallet. Think Web3 service firms, agencies and exporters.{{A}} Much of that B2B USDT probably moves on Tron,{{A}} which is why Tron payment pages come first.

Price

A founding pilot at US$300 for 30 days, then US$200 a month. Both numbers are placeholders to test, not a validated price.{{A}}

How big, bottom-up

If 500 to 2,000 businesses in Singapore and Hong Kong get paid directly in stablecoins often enough to care,{{G}} at US$2,400 a year that is US$1.2–4.8 million in annual revenue. That is a small business, not a venture-scale one. Scale needs more regions, larger customers, payment companies paying per exception,{{G}} or the network and standard in figure 6.

Unit economics

Running costs are a paid chain-data provider, perhaps US$50–300 a month per deployment at pilot volumes,{{G}} plus one container per customer; support time is unmeasured. US$200 a month leaves no room for a sales team: founder-led outreach now, partners (accountants, payment companies) later.{{A}} The cost of winning a customer is unknown.

Section 11

11Conclusion

I study accounting, and I build software for crypto payments. Seen from both sides, one thing is obvious: the money moves well, and everything a business needs after the transfer does not. Clearline is my narrow attempt at that missing layer for businesses paid straight into their own wallets. It gives precise instructions, runs deterministic checks, matches conservatively, explains plainly, and posts entries only with a person's approval, without ever holding funds.

The demo works end to end; live mode is thinner, and Xero hasn't run on a real organisation yet. I know what to build next. What I don't know is whether finance teams feel this problem often enough to pay for it. That is what I am finding out next, one conversation at a time.

A. Glossary

StablecoinA token designed to hold a steady value, usually US$1. USDC (Circle) and USDT (Tether) are the largest.
Token contractThe program on the blockchain that defines a token. Only the issuer's contract makes a token genuine.
Network (chain)A blockchain such as Ethereum or Tron. EVM networks (Ethereum, Polygon, BNB Chain and others) share one address format.
Transaction ID (hash)A unique identifier used to look up a transaction.
Confirmations, finalityBlocks added after a transaction. "Finalized" means validators have made it practically irreversible.
Address poisoningA scam that plants a look-alike address in a victim's history, hoping they copy it later.
Posting planThe exact lines Clearline would write to the books for one invoice, shown before anyone approves.
Idempotency keyA label on a write, so sending it twice has the same effect as sending it once.
Read-onlyAble to look, not to act.
